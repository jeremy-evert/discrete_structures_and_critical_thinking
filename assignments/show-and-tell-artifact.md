## What Show & Tell is

Show & Tell is public explanation and defense under questions — not
presentation theater. On Show & Tell weeks (Weeks 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14), each
student makes a claim, conclusion, model, proof, or technical judgment
public, defends it under audience questions, and gives at least one
professional, specific critique of a peer's reasoning.

## Four distinct things this week produces

1. **The week's technical artifact** — the proof, model, simulation, code,
   representation, derivation, graph, truth table, or evidence set. This is
   the week's normal technical / Reasoning Odyssey work and is graded there.
   Show & Tell does not require a duplicate submission of it.
2. **The live public defense** — presenting the claim and artifact to the
   audience and responding to questions/challenges in the moment.
3. **The individual Show & Tell report** — the compact write-up described
   below. This is the graded Show & Tell object.
4. **The professional critique given to a peer** — required of every
   participating student, captured inside the same report.

## Student-facing report

Submit one compact, individual report per Show & Tell event. Do not restate
the technical artifact; capture what happened when the reasoning became
public.

**About your own claim:**

1. **Claim defended** — what you claimed or concluded, and the strongest
   evidence/proof/model/test that supported it.
2. **Strongest challenge received** — the question, objection,
   counterexample, assumption, or boundary a peer raised that mattered most.
3. **What survived or changed** — what you preserved, strengthened,
   narrowed, corrected, rejected, or left unresolved after the challenge, and
   what your evidence still does not justify.

**About a peer's claim:**

4. **Professional critique you gave** — the claim, assumption, evidence,
   inference, or model you challenged or questioned in someone else's work,
   and why that question was useful (what uncertainty, risk, missing
   evidence, or reasoning boundary it was meant to expose).

These four prompts compress the full seven-part semantic contract (claim,
strongest evidence, strongest challenge received, what survived/changed,
evidence boundary, critique given, why the critique was useful) without
losing meaning. Do not expand this into seven separate bureaucratic scoring
rows.

## The critique norm

> Critique the claim, assumption, evidence, inference, or model. Do not
> attack the reasoner.

Agreeing with a peer is not an exemption from giving critique — probe
whether the evidence deserves the confidence claimed. Revising a claim under
challenge is not failure; refusing to revise when evidence demands it is not
strength.

## Small scoring contract

| Criterion | Full-credit evidence |
|---|---|
| Public claim and evidence | The claim and its strongest supporting evidence are stated clearly enough for the audience to inspect. |
| Response to challenge | The strongest audience challenge is captured honestly, with what survived, changed, or remains unresolved. |
| Evidence-boundary awareness | The report names what the evidence does not yet justify. |
| Professional critique given | The critique of a peer targets an inspectable claim, assumption, evidence, inference, or model — not the person — and states why it was useful. |
| Individual accountability | The report is the student's own account, distinct from the shared technical artifact. |

Do not reward polished speaking style for its own sake, disagreement for its
own sake, or rhetorical dominance.

## Relationship to Pair Reasoning

Pair Reasoning asks what happened to a student's reasoning when another
reasoner worked closely inside the problem with them. Show & Tell asks what
happened when a student made reasoning public, defended it under questions,
and professionally challenged someone else's reasoning. Use the Pair
Reasoning report (`assignments/pair-reasoning-report.md`) for the former.
