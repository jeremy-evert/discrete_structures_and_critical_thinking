## What we covered

Today's class focused on probability concepts, specifically addressing the gambler's fallacy through various examples and discussions. We started by exploring fair hypothetical coin flips to understand independent events and their probabilities. The instructor used a whiteboard alternative to demonstrate how the likelihood of consecutive tails or heads decreases exponentially with each additional flip.

We then delved into Bayesian statistics using an example involving a bag containing one evil coin (with two heads) and 99 fair coins. This helped illustrate how prior beliefs can be updated based on observed evidence, leading us to conclude that after seeing multiple heads in a row, the probability of holding the evil coin increases.

We also ran short Python simulations of the coin streaks and checked what they showed against the calculation (about five heads in a row happening roughly 1 time in 32). The class then covered expected values when rolling a six-sided die, emphasizing that while each face is equally likely, the expected value (the long-run average) is 3.5. We discussed how this concept applies to real-world scenarios like spam filters and wartime code-breaking using Bayesian methods.

Finally, we touched on the historical context of Bayes' theorem, highlighting its significance in modern probability theory despite being published only after his death, when a friend found his essay and sent it to the Royal Society.

## Key ideas

- **Gambler's Fallacy**: The misconception that past random events influence future outcomes. For example, flipping a fair coin multiple times does not increase the likelihood of tails after several heads.
  
- **Bayesian Probability**: Updating beliefs based on evidence. If you observe a series of heads, it increases the probability that you are dealing with an unfair (evil) coin rather than a fair one.

- **Expected Value**: Calculating long-term averages in probabilistic scenarios. For instance, rolling a die repeatedly will average out to 3.5 over many trials, even though individual rolls can vary widely.
