When does recursion fit?

Recursion is a way to describe a problem in terms of smaller versions of itself. Sometimes that makes the structure clearer. Sometimes repeated work or call depth gets in the way. Explore three stories and compare what the computer has to do.

Plain recursion takes exponential time. Memoization makes the work O(n), at the cost of a cache. Both recursive approaches still have a call-depth limit.

03

Sorting shows recursion's sweet spot

Bubble sort makes adjacent passes. Merge sort splits the list, sorts the smaller pieces, then merges them. The built-in sort is the practical Python choice.

Why merge sort fits

Sorting each half is the same kind of task on a smaller input.

The sorted runs will appear here.
Read the structure: split until a list has one item, then combine neighboring sorted lists. Bubble sort is simpler to trace, but its worst-case work grows quadratically. Merge sort grows about n log n and uses extra memory.

Recursion is a natural fit when a problem breaks into smaller problems of the same kind. It is less appealing when the calls repeat work or become too deep.

Three takeaways

Every recursive solution needs a base case and progress toward it. Recursion can make divide-and-conquer structure visible. The call pattern matters: repeated subproblems need a strategy such as memoization, and deep call chains can hit language limits.
