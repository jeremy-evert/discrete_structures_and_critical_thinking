DSCT · Week 6 · Open #01 (before Big O growth)

Big O, one burrito at a time.

In class we built a peanut butter and banana burrito algorithm, timed every step, and accidentally derived Big O. Here it is again, slowly, so you can push on it.

1 · The recipe, as the class timed it from class

A student gave the steps ("burrito bread" was their phrase for the tortilla, and it stuck). The class put a time on each one:

Bread, spread, banana: every burrito needs all three, so they repeat once per burrito. That is 31 seconds each.

The peanut butter jar is different. You open it once, leave it open while you work, and close it at the end. It does not matter whether you make 1 burrito or a million.

Predict first: if you make 100 burritos, how many times do you open the jar?

Once. Same as for 1 burrito, or 2, or 20. That is what "constant" means: the cost does not grow with n.

2 · Turn the dial: n = number of burritos illustrative

The class never timed the jar, so its time is your setting. Push it up to 300 seconds and see whether the answer to the big question changes.

3 · From burritos to the formal statement

Constant time, O(1): the jar. One open, one close, whether n is 1 or a million. Its cost never gets worse as n grows.

Linear time, O(n): the assembly. Total time is about jar + n × (1 + 10 + 20) seconds. Double the burritos and the assembly time doubles: a straight line.

What wins as n grows: the per-burrito work. The fixed jar cost becomes a rounding error next to n copies of the banana step. The whole factory is O(n).

Why the class kept only the banana. To keep the conversation simple, the class kept the single biggest step (20 s) and ignored the 1 s and 10 s. The banana is the largest term, so it dominates the per-burrito cost. The cleaner reason all three can be dropped as far as O(n) goes is that a constant factor (31 s or 20 s per burrito) changes the slope of the line, not its shape. Either way, the answer is linear.

Upper limits. Big O is a worst-case curve: real performance may sit below it, but should not rise above its shape.

Ask the room: what would have to change about the recipe to make the factory not O(n)?

4 · What is real, and what isn't

  • From class: 1 s for the bread, 10 s for the peanut butter, 20 s for the banana (chosen from a 10–20 s range the class looked up); the jar is opened once; the banana is the step that dominates.
  • Jeremy's mental math in class: 10 bananas at 20 s is 200 s. That covers the banana step only. The full per-burrito time for 10 burritos is 310 s, and the table above shows both.
  • A live wobble, kept honest: while explaining the jar, Jeremy said "constant" and "linear" in the same breath and then corrected himself. The clean version is above: one-time jar is constant, per-burrito work is linear.
  • Not from class: the jar's duration (you set it), and the chart. No student names appear on this page; contributions are paraphrased.
