# Can We Trust the Numbers?

Series: When Randomness Fools Us, Part 3. Instructor listening edition. October 20, 2026.

**DANA:** Welcome to the final episode of When Randomness Fools Us. I'm Dana, and today we're going to assemble the whole Week Ten picture. Dobelli helped us recognize two seductive mistakes. The gambler's fallacy invents a balancing force in independent trials. Base-rate neglect forgets the population that a specific observation came from. Rosen supplies a mathematical language for sample spaces, probability, conditioning, and expected value. But we're teaching more than calculation. We're teaching students to decide what the calculation does, and does not, justify.

**MARIA:** I'm Maria. Let's start with the difference between a probability and a decision. Suppose a classroom game uses a fair coin. If it lands heads, you receive one dollar. If it lands tails, you receive nothing. The expected payout is half of one dollar plus half of zero dollars. That's fifty cents per play. Now suppose the game costs sixty cents to enter. Expected net result: negative ten cents per play. No advanced algebra is needed to see the point. A game that sometimes pays can still have a negative average return.

**DANA:** Expected value is central to Rosen section seven point four. For a discrete random variable, list the possible values, multiply each value by its probability, and add the results. But be careful about what the word expected means. It isn't the amount you should predict for the next individual trial. Nobody actually receives fifty cents in our game. Each play produces either zero dollars or one dollar before the entry fee. Fifty cents is a weighted average over the model's possible payouts.

**MARIA:** A student might object, I won a dollar on the first toss, so your expected value was wrong. That's a lovely teachable objection. Expected value does not guarantee the next outcome. It describes the average per trial that repeated observations should tend toward under the model's assumptions. And even that long-run statement isn't a personal promise. Someone playing five times can finish ahead or behind. A one-time decision may also depend on risk, resources, and consequences, not just the mean.

**DANA:** Now let us change the rules. The coin is not fair, and heads arrives sixty percent of the time. The same one-dollar-or-zero payout now has expected gross value sixty cents. If the entry fee is sixty cents, expected net value is zero. No balancing force has intervened. We changed the probability distribution. A mathematical answer moves when a real assumption moves. That is the central message to preserve from today. Changing a number in a model can be responsible reasoning if we can justify the change. Hiding the change is not.

**MARIA:** Here's an example of a misleading simulation. Imagine that a student models a carnival drawing. They put ten virtual tickets in a bag, three of them labeled prize, and each simulated draw chooses a ticket then replaces it. Their program estimates the chance of winning a prize on each draw at thirty percent. So far, fine. But the actual rules say each ticket is removed after it is drawn. A second draw is not from the same ten tickets. Their code can run a million trials and produce beautifully stable statistics. It is still simulating the wrong game.

**DANA:** This is why the Week Ten repository says that agreement between a calculation and a simulation checks the implementation of a shared model, not necessarily the assumptions behind the model. Two people can agree because they copied the same mistake. A calculator may confirm arithmetic while the population is wrong. A program can reproduce the code's rules with exquisite precision while those rules misrepresent reality. In a critical-thinking course, the assumption deserves its own place on the page, not a tiny invisible footnote.

**MARIA:** Let's design a miniature investigation students could defend on Thursday. Start with the coin streak from episode one. Claim: after six tails on a fair independent coin, the probability of heads on the next toss is still one half. Evidence one is a short conditional-probability argument using independence. Evidence two could be a simple script. Repeat an experiment consisting of six tails followed by a seventh toss. Among the runs that begin with six tails, estimate the fraction whose seventh flip is heads. As the number of qualifying trials grows, it should approach one half.

**DANA:** But here is a small engineering snag. Six tails are rare. Only about one out of sixty-four seven-flip trials begins with six tails in our ideal model. If we simulate a few hundred seven-flip sequences, we may have only a handful that qualify. The estimate for the seventh toss will be noisy. A student could instead directly simulate only the next independent flip because, under the model, the previous six outcomes supply no new information. Or they could simulate many more full histories and report how many qualifying histories they actually observed.

**MARIA:** I especially like that distinction. Simulating a conditional question by filtering on a rare event can need much more data than a naive student expects. The denominator is back. A responsible report would say how many simulated trials qualified, not just the percentage that happened to produce heads. This lets you connect episode one to episode two. Coin histories and medical screening tables look different, but both force us to ask which cases belong in the denominator.

**DANA:** Let's build another defensible Week Ten artifact, using the screening example. Claim: in a hypothetical population with one percent prevalence and a test with ninety percent sensitivity and specificity, the chance of the condition given a positive test is about eight point three percent. The artifact can be a four-cell count table. No Python required. It includes nine true positives, one false negative, ninety-nine false positives, and eight hundred ninety-one true negatives. Students should check the grand total and the one hundred eight positive-result denominator.

**MARIA:** Then a peer asks, what if prevalence were ten percent instead? The group rebuilds the table and arrives at fifty percent, using the same test performance. What survived the challenge? The original calculation was valid for the original hypothetical population. What changed? The base rate, and with it the conclusion. What still isn't justified? A claim about any particular real-world disease, test, or population without verified evidence for the actual rates. That is a very good public defense, even without slides or fancy visuals.

**DANA:** The Week Ten Decision Gate asks for five sections. Let's make them sound human. Sources: where did your numbers or rules come from? Rules and assumptions: what must be true for your method to apply? Work: show the model, table, calculation, or reproducible simulation. Check: verify totals, independently recalculate, change one assumption, or compare methods. One-sentence summary: make a claim no bigger than the evidence. If students use AI, they identify how and still check the reasoning themselves. This is a portable habit, not paperwork for paperwork's sake.

The mathematics should be the demanding part, not an archaeological expedition through conflicting instructions.

Protect the important center. You can open with the gambler's fallacy for five or six minutes, then turn the class to the screening table. Let students do the base-rate variation themselves, and use the closing minutes to ask what a simulation can verify. If the period really is seventy-five minutes, trim preview and transition time before cutting the independent reasoning exercise.

**MARIA:** I want to tie all three episodes to one question: when does history matter? In the casino example, knowing previous results does not change the next fair, independent trial. In the screening example, background frequencies matter enormously because they describe how the population is composed. In a drawing without replacement, previous draws matter because the sample space changes. In an expected-value game, the costs and payout distribution matter because probability is connected to consequences. History, background, mechanisms, and stakes each play a different role.

**DANA:** That's also where critical thinking becomes more interesting than a collection of fallacy names. A student might memorize that the gambler's fallacy is bad and then mistakenly dismiss every historical pattern as irrelevant. That's not an improvement. The mature response is to ask whether independence has been justified. Another student might memorize that base rates matter and blindly impose an outdated population statistic on a new setting. Again, wrong lesson. We need evidence about the current population, not the ceremonial presence of a number.

**MARIA:** Here are three short questions you can ask on your way into class. Number one: what is the probability of heads after six tails, assuming an unchanged, fair independent coin? One half. Number two: why might a positive result on a ninety-percent-sensitive and ninety-percent-specific test mean only about an eight-percent chance of the condition? Because the condition may be rare and false positives can outnumber true positives. Number three: what does a simulation prove? It can check behavior under the coded assumptions. By itself, it does not establish that those assumptions match the real world.

**DANA:** Your one-sentence takeaway for today is this. Mathematics tells us what follows from a model; critical thinking asks whether the model deserves our trust. Keep both halves together, and Week Ten becomes more than a lesson about chance. It becomes training in how to make a claim, expose its assumptions, check the work, and stop precisely where the evidence stops. Thanks for listening to When Randomness Fools Us.
