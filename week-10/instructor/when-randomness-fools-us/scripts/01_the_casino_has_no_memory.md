# The Casino Has No Memory

Series: When Randomness Fools Us, Part 1. Instructor listening edition. October 20, 2026.

**DANA:** Welcome to When Randomness Fools Us. I'm Dana, and across three short conversations we're getting ready to teach Discrete Structures and Critical Thinking. Today we're starting with a rather rude discovery. The universe does not owe us a balancing act. If a coin lands tails six times, it isn't preparing an apology. And if a roulette wheel lands black twenty times, the wheel hasn't been keeping score. By the end, I want you to be able to explain both the mathematics and the very human mistake behind the gambler's fallacy.

**MARIA:** I'm Maria. And I want to begin with a little scene. You are walking into class. On the board, you draw six circles. Inside each circle, you write the letter T. T, T, T, T, T, T. Six tails in a row. You ask your students, if the next toss uses a fair coin, is heads now more likely, less likely, or equally likely? Don't tell them to calculate yet. Let the room feel the pull of the word due. Because due is exactly the problem.

**DANA:** Rolf Dobelli makes this the focus of chapter twenty-nine of The Art of Thinking Clearly, the gambler's fallacy. His chapter opens with the familiar Monte Carlo roulette story. A long run of black turns into a crowd convinced that red simply must arrive. This is a story about reasoning, not a recommendation to gamble. The mistake is assuming that nature carries a little ledger with columns for heads and tails, black and red. When one column gets ahead, we imagine some invisible accountant rushing to settle the balance.

**MARIA:** Now put on the Rosen glasses. In Kenneth Rosen's Discrete Mathematics and Its Applications, eighth edition, chapter seven introduces discrete probability. The first job is to describe the sample space. For one ideal fair coin toss, the sample space has two outcomes: heads and tails. Each has probability one half. That doesn't require any knowledge of what happened yesterday, ten seconds ago, or during the previous six flips. We have defined a fresh trial and assigned its probabilities.

**DANA:** Let's distinguish a surprising past from a changed future. The probability of six specific tails on six independent, fair tosses is one half multiplied by itself six times. That's one sixty-fourth, a little over one and a half percent. The fact that the sequence was unlikely before we saw it does not make a head more likely after we have seen it. Conditional on those six tails already having happened, the chance of heads on the next fair, independent toss remains one half.

**MARIA:** This is the sentence to say slowly to students. An unlikely history is not evidence of a compensating future when the trials are independent. The word independent does real mathematical work. For two events, independence means that knowing one event happened does not change the probability of the other. In notation, the probability of B given A equals the probability of B, provided A has positive probability. For our example, A means the first six flips were tails. B means the seventh flip is heads. The fair coin does not consult A.

**DANA:** Here's a sneaky question. Is it wrong to say that a head will probably appear eventually? Not at all. Suppose you get six more independent flips of the fair coin. The probability of seeing at least one head somewhere in those six is one minus the probability of six tails. That's sixty-three sixty-fourths. Very likely. But you have just asked a different question. At least one head somewhere in six opportunities is not the same event as heads on the very next opportunity. The gambler's fallacy often slips between questions without announcing the switch.

**MARIA:** Think of the board again. You write, next flip, fifty percent. Then you write, at least one head in the next six flips, ninety-eight point four percent. Both statements can be correct at the same time. You haven't discovered a cosmic balancing force. You've changed the sample space, the event, and the number of opportunities. That's a beautiful moment for students because it demonstrates why mathematical vocabulary matters. A little ambiguity in ordinary English can hide a large change in the probability model.

**DANA:** There's another trap, the way people talk about the law of large numbers. We expect the fraction of heads to get close to one half over very many independent, fair flips. But that does not mean the counts must catch up after every losing streak. Imagine we've already seen twenty tails and zero heads. As we keep tossing, the fraction of heads may approach one half even if the original twenty-tail gap never closes. The effect of those first twenty on the overall fraction gets smaller as the denominator grows. Averages can stabilize without the past being erased.

**MARIA:** Let's make that tangible. Suppose we started with twenty tails. Then we toss two hundred more times and happen to get a hundred heads and a hundred tails. Total heads: one hundred. Total tails: one hundred twenty. The counts have not balanced. But the fraction of heads is one hundred out of two hundred twenty, roughly forty-five percent, much closer to fifty than we began. The point isn't that this exact later sequence is guaranteed. It isn't. The point is that a diminishing relative imbalance does not imply a special mechanism favoring the underrepresented outcome.

**DANA:** And now the qualification that turns this from a party trick into critical thinking. Independence is not something we can simply chant into existence. In a deck of cards drawn without replacement, the probabilities change as cards leave the deck. If a bag contains two red balls and two blue balls, and you draw one red ball without replacement, the next draw has a different mix than the first. A real roulette wheel might be physically biased. A real coin might not be perfectly fair. Weather, machines, people, and financial systems have memory and feedback. If a process changes, earlier observations can be informative.

**MARIA:** Exactly. The correct principle is not, the past never matters. It's, under a model of independent trials with unchanged probabilities, the past does not change the next trial's probability. Now the class has a testable question. Is independence a reasonable assumption in this situation? Can we examine the mechanism? Have we silently mixed with-replacement and without-replacement experiments? In the DSCT Week Ten repository, that is the critical-thinking question tucked inside the mathematics: how does false independence lead to false confidence?

**DANA:** Let's give students two contrasting mini-cases. Case one: a well-shuffled deck, draw a card, replace it, reshuffle, repeat. Under an ideal model, the probability of drawing an ace each time is four out of fifty-two, or one thirteenth. Case two: draw an ace, keep it out, then draw again. Now there are three aces among fifty-one cards. The second probability is three fifty-firsts. The past matters, because the population of possible outcomes changed. Same word, card. Different rules. Different probability.

**MARIA:** Notice what we just did. We did not ask a machine for a number and stop. We identified a source and a process. We stated the rules and assumptions. We calculated. We checked whether those assumptions matched the physical experiment. And now we can say what the result means. That's your course's five-part reasoning pattern: sources, rules and assumptions, work, check, and one-sentence summary. It works beautifully for simple coin flips and later for dangerous real-world decisions.

**DANA:** I want to add a simulation thought experiment. Suppose a student writes a short Python program that flips an imaginary fair coin ten thousand times and counts the longest run of tails. A streak of six, seven, or more tails might surprise them. But randomness includes clumps and streaks. A simulator isn't defective merely because its results look untidy. Ask the student to write down the actual code's rule. Did each flip independently choose between heads and tails? Did the program accidentally reuse a previous outcome? Did it set a probability other than one half?

**MARIA:** A reproducible simulation can reveal bugs in our expectations. It can also contain bugs of its own. If ten thousand simulated flips produce about half heads, that is consistent with the model, but it does not prove that a casino's wheel is fair. If the code accidentally makes outcomes dependent, a big sample can faithfully reproduce a bad rule. More simulated trials reduce random noise under the model. They do not validate the world that model claims to represent. That distinction will matter again in episode three.

**DANA:** Here's your optional opening activity for today. Put the six tails on the board, and ask for three written responses. First, give the probability of heads on the next flip. Second, state the assumption required. Third, describe one situation where the past really would change the probability. A strong answer says one half, independent and fair trials, and something like drawing cards without replacement. Suddenly you have mathematical precision and an argument students can defend.

**MARIA:** To close, let's circle back to Dobelli. The gambler's fallacy is not really a problem of arithmetic. Many people can multiply one half by one half. The problem is the story we tell when a sequence feels unfair. Our brains want a plot. Mathematics asks for an event, a sample space, and a rule. Chapter twenty-nine helps us notice the seductive story. Rosen gives us the tools to test it. Both belong in the classroom.

**DANA:** Take this sentence with you as the final sound of episode one. A streak tells us what happened. It does not tell us what must happen next, unless we have evidence that the process itself has changed. In our next episode, we'll examine a different kind of mistaken certainty. A test can be ninety percent accurate, and a positive result can still mean an eight percent chance of the condition. That's not a contradiction. It's conditional probability asking us to pay attention to the denominator.
