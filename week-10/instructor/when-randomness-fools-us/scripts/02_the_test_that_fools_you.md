# The Test That Tells the Truth but Fools You

Series: When Randomness Fools Us, Part 2. Instructor listening edition. October 20, 2026.

**DANA:** Welcome back to When Randomness Fools Us. I'm Dana. Last time, our casino had no memory. Today, we're going to meet a test that sounds impressively accurate but gives us a result people are likely to misunderstand. This is the world of base rates and conditional probability. Rolf Dobelli's chapter twenty-eight of The Art of Thinking Clearly is called Base-Rate Neglect. Rosen gives us the formal tools in chapter seven, especially probability theory and Bayes' theorem. Your Week Ten lesson brings them together in a screening-test example.

**MARCUS:** I'm Marcus, and I have a claim for you. A screening test is ninety percent sensitive and ninety percent specific. A person tests positive. Therefore, there is a ninety percent chance that the person has the condition. It sounds reasonable. A good test, a positive result, a confident conclusion. But as your Tuesday run-of-show warns, we've quietly reversed a conditional probability. Sensitivity describes the probability of a positive result when the person has the condition. Our conclusion asks for the probability of the condition when the result is positive. Those are different questions.

**DANA:** Let's say the distinction in plain English. If someone has the condition, how often does the test catch it? That's sensitivity, which we're calling ninety percent. If someone does not have the condition, how often does the test correctly return negative? That's specificity, also ninety percent. But once I know the result is positive, what I want is to compare all the people who tested positive. How many of those actually had the condition? We need an additional piece of information: how common the condition was in the population before testing.

**MARCUS:** That last piece is the base rate. Dobelli's chapter makes a broad psychological point. We are captivated by vivid, specific details and can ignore the boring background distribution. A rare explanation can feel compelling because it fits a description. But our confidence ought to depend on how much of the population belongs to each category. Instead of arguing about which description sounds convincing, we can count. And counting is what your students were practicing in Week Nine.

**DANA:** Build a town of one thousand imaginary people. This is an invented population for teaching, not a claim about a real medical condition. Suppose one percent have the condition. That's ten people with it and nine hundred ninety without it. Our hypothetical test catches ninety percent of the ten, so nine of those ten test positive. One tests negative. Among the nine hundred ninety people without the condition, the test correctly clears ninety percent, which means ten percent get false positives. That's ninety-nine false positives.

**MARCUS:** Now picture a table with two rows and two columns. Rows: condition and no condition. Columns: positive and negative. In the condition-positive cell, put nine. In condition-negative, put one. In no-condition-positive, put ninety-nine. And in no-condition-negative, put eight hundred ninety-one. Before we go anywhere, check the totals. Nine plus one is ten. Ninety-nine plus eight hundred ninety-one is nine hundred ninety. All four cells add to one thousand. That total check is modest, but it catches a shocking number of mistakes.

**DANA:** Now ask the question we actually care about. Out of all positive tests, how many people have the condition? Nine true positives plus ninety-nine false positives gives one hundred eight positive results. Only nine of those people have the condition. Nine divided by one hundred eight is one twelfth, or approximately eight point three percent. A positive result does not mean ninety percent certainty in this scenario. It means about eight percent. The test didn't suddenly become dishonest. We were asking the wrong conditional question.

**MARCUS:** I'd love to pause the recording here, because this deserves to land. Ninety percent sensitivity. Ninety percent specificity. Eight point three percent probability of the condition given a positive result. All three can be true. The one percent base rate explains how. There were so many more people without the condition that even a relatively small false-positive rate generated more positive tests than the test's true positives. When the population is lopsided, the denominator is where most of the story lives.

**DANA:** This is where Rosen's Bayes' theorem enters the room. It is a way of reversing conditional direction while accounting for the base rate. In spoken terms: probability of the condition given a positive result equals probability of a positive result given the condition, times the prior probability of the condition, divided by the total probability of a positive result. You don't need to have students memorize that entire sentence at the beginning. The four-cell table embodies the same reasoning and keeps every population visible.

**MARCUS:** Our Week Ten instructor notes insist on keeping the two conditionals distinct. The probability of positive given condition is not generally equal to the probability of condition given positive. A useful everyday comparison is this: almost every square is a rectangle, in fact every square is a rectangle. But not every rectangle is a square. Knowing the direction of an implication matters. Conditional probabilities are not implications, of course. The analogy just helps us feel how reversing the order can change the meaning.

**DANA:** Let's run the experiment again. Keep the test exactly the same, but change the prevalence from one percent to ten percent. In a population of one thousand, one hundred now have the condition. The test correctly flags ninety of them. Nine hundred do not have it, and the ten percent false-positive rate creates ninety false positives. We now have one hundred eighty positive results, of which ninety are true. Ninety divided by one hundred eighty is fifty percent. Same sensitivity. Same specificity. Different base rate. Very different answer.

**MARCUS:** The point isn't that our first answer was wrong. It's that the answer belongs to a specified population and a specified model. The prevalence matters. Even more importantly, the prevalence among people who actually take the test may differ from prevalence in the general population. A test offered only to people with a specific symptom is being used in a different population from a mass screening program. We should not lift one base rate and casually paste it into every situation. That's exactly the sort of hidden assumption your course wants students to expose.

**DANA:** We can now connect Dobelli twenty-eight to Dobelli twenty-nine. In the gambler's fallacy, we invent a dependency that isn't in our ideal independent-trial model. In base-rate neglect, we leave out information that is already part of the model. They are different mistakes, but both arise because a strong intuition gets promoted to a mathematical conclusion before we state our assumptions. In one case we imagine memory in a random process. In the other we forget the size of the background populations.

**MARCUS:** Let's make the AI fluency opener especially sharp. Ask an AI, or ask a student pretending to be an AI, to explain why a positive test means ninety percent certainty. The polished but wrong answer might say, the test is ninety percent accurate, therefore the positive result is ninety percent reliable. The critical response is not just, that's wrong. It's, which conditional probability do you mean? What's the prevalence? What's the false-positive rate? Can you show the actual counts? We are asking for evidence that can be checked, not confidence that can be admired.

**DANA:** Your class can test the model with a very small exercise. Give the four-cell table but hide the bottom-right value. Ask students to fill it in and independently check the totals. Then ask them to change only the prevalence and rebuild the table. Require them to explain why ninety percent sensitivity does not imply ninety percent probability after a positive test. If they can do that in a few calm sentences without reaching for slogans, they have understood the mathematical heart of the lesson.

**MARCUS:** What about a computer simulation? We can tell the computer to invent one thousand people, tag one percent as having the condition, and simulate test outcomes with the given probabilities. The results will bounce around from run to run. That's expected. If we simulate a much larger population or repeat many runs, the simulated proportions should approach the values predicted by the model. But agreement cannot tell us whether the real-world prevalence is one percent or ten percent. Both the formula and the simulation may share the same mistaken assumption.

**DANA:** Here is the five-part reasoning pattern again. Sources: the population size and test performance rates. Rules and assumptions: prevalence, sensitivity, specificity, and the population we're talking about. Work: the four-cell table and the fraction of positive tests that are true. Check: add every row and column, recompute the denominator, and try another base rate. One-sentence summary: under these hypothetical assumptions, a positive test corresponds to about eight point three percent probability of the condition. Not ninety percent. No drama required.

**MARCUS:** Take one question into the classroom: when someone gives you a percentage, what exactly is in the denominator? This is the question that rescues us from an astonishing number of persuasive errors. It turns a vague claim into something inspectable. It also makes tomorrow's arguments and next week's new mathematical structures more manageable. We have a habit now: don't admire the answer until you know the question it actually answers.
