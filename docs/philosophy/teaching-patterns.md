# Teaching Patterns Evident in the Archive

The archive shows a practice-centered course. Students repeatedly solve, implement, show evidence, and explain rather than only selecting answers. The instructor's recurring homework rubric asks for a summary of the question, sources/resources, assumptions, work, a check of the answer, and a final concise explanation. Programming exams use a parallel structure: a running program, outputs demonstrating behavior, and a written explanation/reflection.

The framing is unusually process-oriented and forgiving. Several finals ask students to choose reflection questions and answer a manageable subset; some work is pass/fail or offers bonus points. Language such as “precision without panic,” “the process for working through a problem,” and “you are welcome to answer as many as you need” signals an emphasis on making thinking visible, encouraging revision/help-seeking, and treating mistakes as part of learning.

The course also connects discrete thinking to agency and employability. Welcome work, degree planning, resume evidence, dream-job research, gap analysis, GitHub/LinkedIn, and professional learning recur in 2023–26. Spring 2026 makes the social learning design explicit: pair programming reports record roles, evidence, a win, a stuck point, and a next step; show-and-tell asks what was shown, learned, and questioned.

These are archive-grounded patterns, not requirements for every future section. The archive does not preserve a stable syllabus, grading policy, or complete feedback bank, so those should be designed separately rather than inferred too strongly.
