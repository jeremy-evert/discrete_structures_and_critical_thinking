## Course description

Introduction to discrete mathematics for computer science: sets, functions, propositional and predicate logic, Boolean algebra, graph theory, matrices, proof techniques, combinatorics, and finite state machines.

## Course purpose

Use discrete mathematics as a laboratory for disciplined reasoning. Students learn to make claims, locate rules/sources, show work, test counterexamples, explain uncertainty, revise conclusions, and use tools including AI without surrendering judgment.

## Required materials and resources

- Canvas access and a reliable way to submit reasoning, code/evidence, and explanations.
- No required textbook or external course for Fall 2026. Course-owned materials and no-cost references are the active path; historical ZyBooks/Rosen material may be used only as optional/reference material.
- A working computing environment for bounded code, simulation, local-AI, and container/LaTeX activities as directed by the course.
- Paid AI subscriptions are not required.

## Course learning goals

1. Construct and evaluate claims using logic, proof, examples, and counterexamples.
2. Use sets, functions, sequences, relations, matrices/digraphs, graphs, and trees as representations.
3. Analyze algorithms, correctness, growth, counting, probability, uncertainty, induction, recursion, and recurrence reasoning.
4. Apply integer properties and cryptographic ideas with explicit assumptions and evidence boundaries.
5. Reason about Boolean algebra, circuits, finite-state machines, and model limits.
6. Use AI, code, simulation, and external sources as tools while preserving independent verification and responsibility for conclusions.

## Learning activities and assignments

- Weekly Decision Gates that package claim, sources/rules, work/evidence, check, and summary reasoning.
- Reasoning Odyssey checkpoints in Weeks 7, 11, and 14. On checkpoint weeks the checkpoint replaces the ordinary Decision Gate.
- Pair Reasoning and Show & Tell work on their scheduled rotation.
- Tuesday AI Fluency / Professional Minds / technical presentation touchpoints.
- Career artifact sequence, Week 16 Farkle + Machine Learning synthesis, and Week 17 final reflection.

Assignments are evidence-bearing work. Students should show what they tried, what happened, and why the evidence supports or challenges the conclusion. Canvas is authoritative for the exact submission location and due timestamp.

## Attendance and participation

## Late work, revision, and recovery

The computing curriculum uses a shared continuous late-work default unless a course source explicitly overrides it. Work is graded for academic quality first. Effective lateness then reduces that earned score continuously at 1% per 24 hours late, including fractional days, after any instructor-granted excused late time. Assignments may remain open after the due date so recovery is still worthwhile, subject to the real end-of-semester grading boundary. Revisions/resubmissions may be accepted where the course workflow permits them; course-specific highest-score and drop-lowest rules in the repository control when applicable. Canvas holds the authoritative due/submission timestamps.

## Artificial intelligence and outside resources

AI is welcome as a learning and investigation tool when the assignment permits it. Students may use AI to brainstorm, explain, compare approaches, debug, revise, or propose code. AI is not the authority for the submitted work. Students remain responsible for accuracy, originality, privacy, and academic integrity. When AI materially contributes, disclose the tool and what it did, then name the independent evidence used to verify the result. A typical disclosure is: ‘I used [tool] for [role]. I verified the result with [test, run, source, calculation, comparison, or explanation].’
