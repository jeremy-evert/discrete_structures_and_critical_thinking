# Week 16 Tuesday: Farkle Evidence Handout

## The job this week

Our question is: **When a Farkle strategy appears better, what are we actually justified in believing?**

Start with the [Week 16 glossary](glossary.md). This handout has everything you need; the slides PDF is linked on the Tuesday landing page if you want the visual version.

This is an evidence lab, not a contest to crown a strategy. **Evidence** means information we can inspect and use to support or limit a claim. A **claim** is a sentence that says what we think is true. Your job is to make the claim no larger than the evidence.

You will not write a Farkle engine, which is a program that implements the game rules. You will not write a machine-learning algorithm, which is a step-by-step method for learning a decision rule from data. You need no special hardware, cloud account, or paid tool. The shared materials provide the game results; you provide the reasoning.

The numbers you will read are practice numbers from a fallback fixture (a ready-made set of results used when live running is not available), produced by the shared course Farkle package; they are not live student results.

## Words before work

These definitions are the working meanings for this handout:

- **Farkle** is a dice game in which a player may bank turn points or risk them by rolling again; a Farkle loses the unbanked turn points.
- A **turn** is one player's opportunity to roll or bank before the opponent's turn. **Banking** means adding the current turn points to that player's total and ending the turn.
- A **strategy** is a fixed rule for choosing an action from the information the strategy can see. A **learner** is a strategy that uses training examples or experience to choose an action.
- A **model** is a simplified representation of a real situation. Here it represents the game, the strategies, and the comparison rules.
- A **simulation** is repeatedly running a model to observe outcomes under stated rules.
- A **sample** is the particular games observed in one run. A **finite** sample has a limited number of observations.
- A **seed** is a label that makes one **pseudo-random** sequence repeatable. Pseudo-random means generated by a repeatable procedure that behaves like random choice for this experiment. Repeatable does not mean **universal**, or true for every case.
- A **win rate** is wins divided by the games counted for that strategy. The **numerator** is the top number in a division, and the **denominator** is the bottom number; here the denominator is the games counted.
- A **percentage point** is a direct difference between two percentages: 70% minus 30% is 40 percentage points. We write **pp** as a short label for percentage points.
- A **sign** tells whether a number is plus or minus. For example, 5 has a plus sign and -3 has a minus sign.
- A **decision rule** must be a test someone else can check. It may include a number **threshold**, meaning the minimum difference or amount required before the rule declares A, declares B, or refuses. A **criterion** is the feature used to judge or order choices; a **metric** is a measurement used for that judgment.
- **Example only — not our answer:** “I will call A better only if A wins more games than B in every row AND the smallest A-minus-B difference is at least 20 points; otherwise I refuse.” A **range** is the largest value minus the smallest value; for the numbers 8, -2 and 5 it is 8 - (-2) = 10.
- The **scope** of a statement is the exact conditions and cases to which it applies. A **bounded** conclusion stays within that scope.
- An **assumption** is something treated as fixed or true for the comparison. An **outcome** is what happened in one game, such as an A win, B win, or **tie**; a tie means neither strategy wins that game.
- **Balanced starts** means each strategy starts the same number of games in the first position and the second position. A **start** records which player takes the first turn.
- **Stability** means that a pattern stays similar across samples or seeds. **Variation** is the way results can change from one sample or seed to another.
- **Machine learning** is a way of producing a rule from examples or experience; it does not make a finite result universal.
- An **overclaim** is a statement stronger or broader than its evidence supports. A **model limitation** is a reason the model cannot answer some larger question.
- A **fallback fixture** is a validated substitute for live execution when live execution fails. It is **synthetic**: made for classroom practice from a stated setup, not from student data.

## The Predict, Check, Explain loop

Use this loop for each move:

1. **Predict:** write what you expect before seeing the next result.
2. **Check:** inspect the supplied counts and rebuild the rates.
3. **Explain:** say what the check supports and what it does not support.

A prediction may be wrong. That is useful because a visible prediction can be checked; an invisible prediction cannot.

## Fixed comparison

Use the comparison shown in the Tuesday slides and supplied evidence:

- **Target score** is the score needed to end the game; here it is 4000.
- Strategy A: `learner:500`. This is a transparent table-based learner: it trains on 500 turns, sorts each situation into a group by how many turn points it has so far and how many dice it can still roll, and then chooses roll or bank from learned average outcomes. The `500` means 500 training turns, not 500 games.
- Strategy B: `bank_at_425`. It banks once the current turn score reaches 425 points; below 425, it rolls again.

## Ask whether another metric changes the story

No extra numbers are supplied for this step; reason in words: would ordering by **points per game** plausibly change your decision, and why? Points per game means the average points scored in one complete game. You may instead discuss **Farkle rate per own turn**, meaning the number of your Farkles divided by the number of turns you took; an own turn is one opportunity for your strategy's player to roll or bank. Preparation effort means the work or time needed before evaluation, evaluation speed means time per game, and simplicity means how few rules or components a strategy requires.

Sample answer: “If I ordered by Farkle rate per own turn, my decision might change because I could prefer fewer Farkles; no extra numbers are supplied, so this is a reasoned possibility, not a measured result.”

### Work

Use the alternate-metric reasoning step above; do not pretend a new metric answers the old question automatically.

### Check Your Answer

An **audit** inspects a claim for its evidence, assumptions, calculations, and limitations. Audit whether a confident reader or AI hides the denominator or ordering criterion, treats one sample as proof, calls a small difference meaningful without a rule, or ignores a model limitation. Write one repaired sentence.

### One-Sentence Summary

Choose one of these final moves under your stated rule:

- **Defend:** keep the original limited claim because the checks support it. Model sentence: “I defend the limited claim that B had the higher observed win rate in seed 17 under these rules.”
- **Revise:** replace the claim with a narrower or different claim because the evidence changed what you can responsibly say. Model sentence: “I revise my claim to say only that B had the higher observed win rate in this one row.”
- **Refuse:** do not declare a winner because the evidence does not satisfy the rule. Model sentence: “I refuse to declare a winner because my rule requires the same strategy to lead in every row.”

Your sentence must include the scope of the observation and one limitation. Refusing to declare a winner can be the precise result when the rule is not satisfied.

## The week's AI failure mode

A confident reader or AI can hide the denominator or the ordering criterion; one sample can be treated as proof. That is an **AI failure mode**, meaning a predictable way an AI-generated or polished explanation can mislead. Audit the argument instead of grading its confidence. Ask: What was counted? What was the denominator? What does “better” mean? Did the ordering stay the same? What can this model not establish?
